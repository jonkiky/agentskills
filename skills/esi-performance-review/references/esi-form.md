# ESI review form

This reference describes the form observed in the local review workflow. Verify the current form when entering text. The employee's chosen topic is part of the context, not a fixed company-wide choice for that field.

| Field | Purpose of the manager comment |
| --- | --- |
| Leadership Principle Achievement | Assess the selected leadership behavior using specific feedback and outcomes. |
| Technical Capability Achievement | Explain the selected technical contribution, engineering practices, and supported impact. |
| My Choice Achievement (LP or Technical) | Cover the selected additional achievement with a distinct emphasis and evidence. |
| Leadership Principle to Improve | Recognize existing strengths and summarize relevant suggestions actually made by reviewers. |
| Technical Capability to Improve | Recognize the current technical foundation and include reviewer suggestions only where supplied. |
| My Choice to Improve (LP or Technical) | Address the selected topic; keep self-identified goals distinct from reviewer suggestions. |
| General Comments | Summarize the overall contribution and the most important reviewer-supported growth suggestions. |

The form asks the employee to select at least one Leadership Principle and one Technical Capability among the three achievements and among the three improvement topics. Manager comments should respond to those existing selections.

A complete review contains these six competency comments plus General Comments. A request for the full review includes all seven fields; a request limited to specific fields stays limited to those fields.

Every comment must contain at least 100 words of body text, including General Comments. Count each field separately and exclude its title, source notes, and count metadata. Aim for 110–150 words using relevant details already supported by the source material. The minimum applies to each field, not each paragraph, and does not justify filler, invented evidence, or new advice.

## Resolving common evidence mismatches

- **The selected principle is supported indirectly.** Describe the actual behavior and its relevance without claiming that a reviewer explicitly named the principle. If the evidence supports scope clarification but not a disagreement, do not invent a debate or opposition to satisfy “Challenge and Commit.”
- **An improvement topic also has strong positive evidence.** Preserve the demonstrated capability. Include a request to apply it more broadly only when a reviewer made that request. Do not claim the employee lacks knowledge that a reviewer specifically praises.
- **The selected topic appears only in the self-review.** Describe the employee's own goal as such. Do not add proposed actions or suggest peer endorsement. Keep any note about missing peer feedback in manager notes, outside the comment.
- **Reviewers praise delivery while requesting stronger leadership.** Preserve both observations: reliable hands-on work can coexist with a request for earlier technical direction, delegation, or broader ownership.
- **Feedback raises competing project demands.** Preserve the reviewer's request to communicate workload so managers can prioritize. Do not invent missed deadlines, add a new reporting schedule, or imply unlimited availability is the desired standard.

## Suggested composition

Write normal paragraphs in casual, respectful language, without bullets, numbering, or bold lead-in labels. Each paragraph opens with a short point and immediately follows it with its own example or explanation. Keep official field and principle names. Use verified project names and useful feature names to make the work clear, while omitting reviewer attribution and other employees' names.

Project names describe where the work happened; they are not labels for the feedback source. Write “On Project A, he…” rather than attributing a statement to that project's reviewer. Keep different projects' work and completion status distinct.

Use direct manager language. Avoid source-reporting phrases such as “the feedback describes,” “the feedback notes,” or “the requested next steps.” State what the employee did and what they should work on. Preserve any relevant timing or uncertainty without referring to the source document. For example, retain that code-review issues occurred during a short period when the employee was new and may have improved since. Do not claim firsthand observation or current problems that the source does not establish.

For achievements, state the contribution and then show what the employee did. For development, acknowledge the current strength and include relevant reviewer suggestions if supplied. Use no “Evidence” label and no shared block of examples at the end. An example belongs immediately after the point it supports, in the same paragraph.

Do not add suggestions just to fill a development field. A self-review goal may be summarized as the employee's own intention, but it is not a reviewer recommendation. Keep source mapping in a separate manager file, not in the draft the employee may see.

## Where to put reviewer improvement requests

Include every distinct improvement request from the feedback in a complete review. Choose its primary location by the substance of the request and the employee's selected topic, rather than treating all suggestions as belonging to one improvement field. Relevant growth advice may follow an achievement in the same category. For example, guiding other engineers can fit Empower Others, and owning a release from start to finish can fit Deliver Outcomes.

If none of the selected topics fits a request naturally, put it in General Comments. Workload and deadline communication across projects may belong there when no selected competency covers that issue. Do not omit the request or stretch an unrelated topic to accommodate it. Keep a source-to-destination coverage record in manager notes and avoid repeating the same detailed advice in multiple categories.

Preserve a request to continue a good practice as a continuation request, not a newly invented weakness.

## Source and form boundaries

Use the standalone ESI Leadership Principles document when supplied. The feedback forms may also append a shorter glossary. Definitions, boundary examples, and scenario stories explain expected behavior; they do not establish that the employee showed that behavior or its opposite.

For the commonly selected principles in this workflow:

- **Challenge and Commit:** Speak up respectfully before a decision and support the chosen direction afterward. Early questions can support the comment, but do not invent a disagreement that the feedback never describes.
- **Deliver Outcomes:** Focus on what the work made possible for the customer or team. Distinguish that result from a list of tasks, and do not invent adoption, satisfaction, or time-saving numbers.
- **Empower Others:** Set clear goals, give people room to make decisions, and offer support. A suggestion to delegate more does not prove that the employee micromanages or holds the team back.

These notes reflect the supplied document and should be checked against it if the source changes. If alignment with a particular Career Guide level is requested, obtain that guide or identify that it is missing; do not imply that peer comments alone establish the formal level requirements.

General Comments is a separate overall write-up, included by default in a complete review. Use one or two natural paragraphs to connect the main contribution, team support, and reviewer suggestions. Include any reviewer improvement that does not fit the selected categories, adding a short paragraph if needed. It must contain at least 100 words, normally aiming for 110–150. Check actual form requirements before entry and surface any conflicting limit. Do not copy all six comments into it, add a rating, or invent new advice. Maintain reviewer privacy and keep its source mapping in manager notes.
